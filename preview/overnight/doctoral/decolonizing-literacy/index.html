Decolonizing Literacy

Challenging English-only norms through methods supporting linguistic justice in multilingual education.

← Back to Doctoral Coursework & Research Projects

Project overview

This focal-point project examines how English-dominant literacy norms can operate as gatekeeping mechanisms that marginalize multilingual and multicultural students. It brings together linguistic justice, dialect studies, translanguaging, multimodality, reading theory, and culturally sustaining practice to ask what literacy can become when standardized print English is no longer treated as the only legitimate form of academic language.

Linguistic diversity is positioned as a foundation for transformative education, not a problem to be corrected.

The problem

English dominance

Dominant literacy norms can narrow what counts as intelligence, rigor, and legitimate academic language.

Linguistic profiling

John Baugh’s work shows how racialized judgments about speech can create barriers before a speaker is ever seen.

Anti-Black linguistic racism

April Baker-Bell’s framework centers the harm created when schools privilege Standard American English while treating AAVE as deficient.

Anglocentric literacy models

The project questions reading and literacy frameworks that universalize features of English instead of accounting for linguistic diversity.

Research foundation

The project synthesizes scholarship and media on narrative power, linguistic profiling, Black language, bilingual education, translanguaging, African American English, reading science, and multimodal meaning-making. The sources are organized around three moves: naming the problem, reviewing the research, and moving toward application.

Selected frameworks and sources

  • Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie: dominant narratives and the danger of allowing one story to define people, cultures, or intelligence.
  • John Baugh: linguistic profiling and discrimination based on perceived racialized speech.
  • April Baker-Bell: linguistic justice, Black language, identity, pedagogy, and the need to dismantle deficit models.
  • Frieson & Presiado: Black liberation and Black freedom dreaming in bilingual education.
  • Lems: translanguaging as a way to understand and use learners’ full linguistic repertoires.
  • Reading Universe / Julie Washington: the relationship between African American English and reading instruction.
  • Share: Anglocentric bias in the science of reading.
  • Geneva Smitherman: Black language, culture, and education.

From research to application

The project does not stop at critique. It connects the scholarship to my earlier work in The Learning Curve and then to Black & Bilingual as a practical space for applying linguistic justice through curriculum, products, and community learning.

Culturally sustaining curriculum

Center translanguaging, diasporic language practices, and community-based knowledge while treating diverse language repertoires as assets.

Community-rooted learning

Develop multilingual learning spaces outside English-dominant constraints that can function as living laboratories for linguistic justice.

Innovative literacy tools

Use bilingual literacy resources and emerging technology to create multilingual learning models that do not default to English-only assumptions.

Liberatory practice

Shift literacy from a gatekeeping mechanism toward a practice that affirms students as globally competent and intellectually rigorous.

Connection to Black & Bilingual

The culminating direction is explicit: Black & Bilingual is presented as a blueprint for linguistic justice. The project positions the company’s curriculum, multilingual resources, diasporic focus, community learning possibilities, and future technology work as ways to put the scholarship into practice.
