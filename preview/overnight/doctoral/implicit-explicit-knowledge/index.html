Implicit & Explicit Knowledge

An examination of how conscious language knowledge and automatic fluency interact—and what the interface debate means for instruction.

← Back to Doctoral Coursework & Research Projects

Project overview

This chapter analysis examines implicit knowledge, explicit knowledge, and three major positions in the interface debate: no-interface, weak interface, and strong interface. It then translates those theoretical positions into classroom implications, including inductive and deductive instruction, consciousness-raising tasks, and dictogloss.

Language rules don’t create fluency, but they light the path that leads to it.

At a glance

No-interface

Implicit and explicit knowledge remain separate; meaningful input and interaction drive spontaneous language use.

Weak interface

Explicit knowledge can support noticing and learning, but its influence depends on developmental readiness and the type of rule.

Strong interface

Practice can move declarative knowledge toward procedural and increasingly automatic performance.

Classroom application

Consciousness-raising and dictogloss create space for noticing, comparison, feedback, fluency, and accuracy.

Teaching implications

The project argues that the interface debate matters because it changes how teachers balance exposure, form-focused instruction, practice, and feedback. Explicit instruction can be deductive or inductive, but the quality of the rule matters: it must balance accuracy, clarity, and simplicity.

Practice design

I developed sample activities for subject–verb agreement and the present simple. The activities require students to notice patterns, reconstruct language, compare their output with a model, explain differences, and use feedback to refine both awareness and performance.
