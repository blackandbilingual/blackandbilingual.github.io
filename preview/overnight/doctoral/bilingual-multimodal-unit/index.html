Bilingual Multimodal Unit Plan

Language, identity, and civic justice in a four-day grades 9–10 bilingual social-studies unit.

← Back to Doctoral Coursework & Research Projects

Project overview

This unit is designed for a grades 9–10 Social Studies/Civic Engagement setting in an additive transitional bilingual program using a content-based pull-out model in Metro Atlanta. The instructional model uses approximately 60% English and 40% students’ home languages, with explicit space for reflective writing, vocabulary comparison, collaboration, and multimodal production.

The unit moves from language and power to argument development and civic action.

Unit goals

Language & identity

Students analyze how language influences identity and participation in civic life.

Bilingualism & access

Students explain how bilingualism can shape representation, voice, access, and participation within communities.

Cross-cultural comparison

Students compare how advocacy, participation, community, and civic voice are expressed in the United States and in their home cultures.

Multimodal civic project

Students create a bilingual, multiliterate project demonstrating understanding of language, identity, and civic responsibility.

Language development

The unit develops academic language through four major functions: narrate, explain, inform, and argue. Key vocabulary includes identity, language, community, participation, civic duty, representation, responsibility, perspective, justice, advocacy, bilingual, multilingual, access, and engagement.

Students are expected to use bilingual written artifacts, collaborative discussion, translanguaging, cross-linguistic comparison, and structured academic discourse to build both content understanding and biliteracy.

Four-day learning arc

Day 1 — Language & Power

Students begin with a bilingual quick write, build civic vocabulary, analyze a civic speech through audience, tone, purpose, and language, participate in structured discussion, and close with a claim-based exit ticket. Community guests are included in the conversation from the beginning.

Day 2 — Language & Social Justice

Students engage with the Passport to Protest mini-lesson, compare justice-related language across languages, write a bilingual comparison, and participate in a mini-debate about language and social justice.

Day 3 — Build the Civic Justice Project

Students select a format from a choice board and develop a speech, video, poster, interview, or another approved multimodal product. Their work must include a civic claim, evidence from a social-justice example, five key vocabulary terms, and meaningful bilingual integration.

Day 4 — Community Presentation & Reflection

Students present to peers and community members, respond to questions and feedback, and complete a bilingual reflection on how bilingualism supports civic responsibility.

Translanguaging by design

The unit intentionally gives students room to use their full linguistic repertoires. Students may draft in a home language before producing in English, build bilingual vocabulary charts, identify cognates, compare culturally specific concepts across languages, work with bilingual peers, and make strategic language choices based on audience and purpose.

Assessment & differentiation

Formative assessment

Bilingual quick writes, vocabulary checks, structured discussion observation, comparison writing, exit tickets, and debate participation.

Summative assessment

The Civic Justice Project assesses argument, academic vocabulary, oral/written communication, evidence, multimodality, and meaningful home-language integration.

Access supports

Visual vocabulary cards, bilingual glossaries, annotated mentor texts, graphic organizers, sentence frames, audio models, and structured peer support.

Authentic audience

Community members participate in the learning process and return as an audience for final presentations, allowing students to use civic language in a real communicative setting.

Black & Bilingual in practice

The unit uses Black & Bilingual’s Passport to Protest as one of its core instructional materials and reflects the larger direction of the company: culturally grounded, diasporic, multilingual learning that connects language to real-world identity, movement, and community participation.
