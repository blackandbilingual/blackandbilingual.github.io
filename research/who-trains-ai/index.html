Who Trains AI Matters

A human-centered, autoethnographic study of culture, labor, and bias on AI's front lines.

Human-Centered AI Algorithmic Bias Community Cultural Wealth AI Labor
Who trains AI?

Culture · Labor · Bias

The inquiry

AI reflects the people shaping it.

Artificial intelligence does not develop in isolation. Human trainers, engineers, reviewers, annotators, voice actors, and writers continuously teach models how to interpret instructions, reward certain responses, and operate in the real world.

This project uses Erica Baker's experience training and evaluating AI systems to examine whose knowledge becomes part of the technology. It asks what happens when culturally narrow workforces train systems intended to serve a global public, and what becomes possible when Black, bilingual, neurodivergent, immigrant, and other historically excluded people help build them.

Diversity is not decoration. It is part of the infrastructure.

Purpose

A call to decolonize AI's front lines.

01

Ethical safeguard

Cultural diversity helps expose assumptions before they become embedded as automated decisions.

02

Technical necessity

Models need human context to understand language, identity, dietary practices, emotion, and everyday life.

03

Economic opportunity

AI work can create new pathways to high-paying, flexible work for people often excluded from tech.

Critical frameworks

Three lenses guide the analysis.

Critical Race Theory

Reveals how historical power structures and inequities can be translated into algorithms, datasets, surveillance, and automated decision-making.

Human-Centered AI

Prioritizes human dignity, agency, safety, and judgment while pairing high human control with useful technological automation.

Community Cultural Wealth

Positions linguistic, familial, social, navigational, aspirational, and resistant knowledge as valuable expertise rather than cultural deficit.

Findings from the front lines

Culture appears in every task.

  1. AI models can miss critical cultural details, including religious dietary practices, food allergies, regional context, and the ways identity shapes daily decisions.
  2. Model failures are opportunities for human experts to identify what was missed and teach systems to respond with greater nuance, accuracy, and respect.
  3. The people doing this work are not invisible support. Their lived knowledge actively determines what AI learns to recognize, prioritize, and protect.
  4. Public conversations must hold both truths: AI can reproduce harm, and responsible participation can create economic mobility, structural equity, and better technology.
