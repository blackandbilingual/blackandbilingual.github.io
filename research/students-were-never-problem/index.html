The Students Were Never the Problem

An epistemic justice analysis of compliance, curriculum, and possibility in an elementary EBD classroom.

Epistemic JusticeSpecial EducationRestorative PracticeProject-Based Learning
Compliance≠Learning

Curriculum · Power · Possibility

The inquiry

What if the barrier lives in the system?

This inquiry grew from Erica's experience leading an elementary Emotional and Behavioral Disorder classroom serving Black and Latinx students with intersecting disabilities and learning needs.

The classroom relied heavily on behavior tracking, scripted lessons, and compliance-based interventions. The study asks who gets recognized as the knower in this environment, whose knowledge is dismissed, and whether the structures intended to support students can instead intensify disengagement and distress.

The contrast in outcomes showed that the barriers were not within the children. They were embedded within the system.

Analytical lenses

Four ways of understanding the classroom.

Positivism

Explains the classroom's dependence on observable behavior, data trackers, standardized expectations, scripted instruction, and adult authority.

Constructivism

Centers relationship building, student choice, collaboration, context, and the ways learners create meaning through lived experience.

Critical Theory

Examines how power and institutional structures position some students as problems to be managed instead of learners capable of knowledge and agency.

Epistemic Justice

Asks whose voice is considered credible and what changes when disabled Black and Brown students are treated as legitimate knowledge makers.

Classroom intervention

From scripted instruction to meaningful work.

Erica replaced passive, grade-level scripted lessons with activity-based learning aligned to academic standards. Students participated in gallery walks, collaborative building, and individualized projects based on their interests.

Interest

Personal relevance

Projects drew from interests such as Roblox, cats, and Spider-Man to create an entry point into academic work.

Agency

Active participation

Students moved, created, collaborated, and made decisions instead of receiving knowledge through a fixed script.

Outcome

Greater engagement

Academic participation increased, while the most extreme expressions of distress decreased.

Implications

Compliance cannot be the educational goal.

  1. EBD classrooms should center constructivist, project-based, and interest-based learning that reflects developmental readiness.
  2. Student behavior should be interpreted within emotional, sensory, cultural, and environmental context rather than treated as an isolated data point.
  3. Restorative and trauma-informed practices should replace disciplinary structures focused solely on obedience.
  4. Students and families should participate in decisions about curriculum, classroom routines, and definitions of success.
